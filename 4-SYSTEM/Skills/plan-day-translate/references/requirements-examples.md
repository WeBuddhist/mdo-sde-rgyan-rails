# Style-contract examples — what a stream's `requirements.md` should contain

**These are examples, not contracts.** Both are drawn from one vault's plans
over the *Bodhisattvacaryāvatāra*. They are reproduced here so that whoever
authors a new stream's `requirements.md` can see the level of specificity that
makes a register rule checkable. **Copy the shape, not the content** — another
text, another audience and another language will need different rules.

Register rules belong in `<lang>/requirements.md`. They are deliberately **not**
in `plan-day-translate`: a skill that carries one language's register rules
cannot serve a second language, and a rule buried in a skill cannot be edited by
the person who owns the stream.

---

## Example A — an English stream aimed at a beginner, non-native audience

### Audience and register

- Reader: lay readers new to the commentarial tradition; many are not native
  English speakers.
- Reading level: CEFR A2–B1.
- One idea per sentence, active voice, under 20 words.
- No honorific archaism. The source language marks respect grammatically;
  English has no honorific register — do not reach for "deign to accept" or
  "supplicate". Carry the respect through plain, warm phrasing.

### Terms and names

Keep genuine terms of art and proper names: bodhicitta, karma, samsara, Dharma,
Sangha. Gloss once per file where the termbase requires it.

Render epithets and descriptive titles plainly:

| Source | Write | Not |
|---|---|---|
| `ས་བཅུའི་དབང་ཕྱུག་` | great bodhisattvas who have reached the highest stages | lords of the tenth bhumi |
| `ཐུགས་རྗེ་ཆེན་པོ་ཅན་` | full of compassion | possessed of great compassion |
| `ཡོན་ཏན་རྒྱ་མཚོ་` | an ocean of good qualities | oceanic assembly of virtues |
| `སྒོ་གསུམ་` | body, speech and mind | the three doors |

The same principle governs ordinary high-frequency vocabulary: "wrongdoing" not
"misdeeds"; "doing good" not "virtue"; "carelessness" not "heedlessness";
"laziness" not "sloth". The scholarly alternative is never the right answer in
this stream.

### Diacritics

None in body prose (Shantideva, Manjushri, bodhicitta). Two exemptions only:
verse quotations reproduced verbatim keep whatever the source has, including
"Mañjuśrī"; and the italicised title of a cited work keeps its diacritics. The
distinction is between a term used in prose and a title being cited.

### Forbidden elements

No em-dashes in body prose. No emojis. No diacritics in prose (above). Light
bold only — a few key phrases, never whole sentences or every paragraph. No
benefits list, no end glossary, no bullet "application" block, no parenthetical
keyword tags, no sub-headings below `##`, no horizontal rules between sections.
No rhetorical question-and-answer. No consequence stated without naming who it
falls on. No importance asserted ("great", "profound") instead of shown by
comparison or result.

### Bands (diagnostics)

| Section | Band |
|---|---|
| introduction | 80–115 words |
| commentary | 180–220 words |
| practice explanation | 120–160 words |
| the practice itself | one sentence |

### Per-section voice

- Introduction: fixed three-move shape — locate the verses, preview the theme,
  invite. Opens with the plan's established formula naming chapter and verses.
- Commentary: third person; attributions preserved exactly as the source states
  them.
- Practice: first person and forward-looking — "Today I will…", never "You
  should…" and never "The practitioner bows…".

## Example B — a Tibetan authoring stream

### Voice, by section

- Verse section: no prose at all — verse text only.
- Commentary section: neutral, explanatory. Not first person. The reader may be
  addressed with ཁྱེད་ for warmth.
- Practice section: first person singular only — ངས་, ང་རང་, བདག་གིས་. Never
  ང་ཚོས་ or ང་ཚོ་. This is the practitioner's own voice.

### Sentence architecture

**Each clause does one thing** — one time reference, one action, or one
conclusion — then closes and the next opens. Never pack two logical functions
into one subordinate clause.

Clause-connection ladder, in order of closeness:

| Particle | Use when… |
|---|---|
| ཞིང་ / ལ་ | two actions happen together or in quick succession |
| ནས་ / ཏེ | the second clause follows from or results from the first |
| དང་། | listing items of equal weight |
| ། | complete stop before a new, independent thought |
| ཡིན་ནོ།། / འགྱུར་རོ།། | close a paragraph with a sense of settled conclusion |

**Never end a paragraph mid-thought.** The final sentence should feel like an
arrival, not a trailing clause.

### Nominalisation trap

Keep modifier chains one level deep; rewrite deeper stacks as two clauses.

❌ ང་རང་གི་མི་རྟག་པའི་བསམ་པ་འདིས་ད་ལྟར་གྱི་གོ་སྐབས་ལ་རྩར་ཆོད་ཅིག་བྱ་རྒྱུ་ཡིན་ནོ།།
✓ མི་རྟག་པར་དྲན་ཞིང་ད་ལྟར་གྱི་གོ་སྐབས་ལ་དམ་འཛིན་བྱ་རྒྱུ་ཡིན་ནོ།།

### Ending particles (རྫོགས་ཚིག/མཐའ་རྟགས)

When a sentence-final verb closes with one of the ten suffix letters
ག ང ད ན བ མ ར ལ ས, the matching ending particle must be used — the initial
consonant of the particle equals the suffix letter:

| Suffix | Particle | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ག | གོ | བྱེད་གོ། |
| ང | ངོ | གཏོང་ངོ་། |
| ད | དོ | བགྱིད་དོ། |
| ན | ནོ | བྱིན་ནོ། |
| བ | བོ | འགྲུབ་བོ། |
| མ | མོ | བསམ་མོ། |
| ར | རོ | བགྱིར་རོ། |
| ལ | ལོ | བསྐྱལ་ལོ། |
| ས | སོ | བགྱིས་སོ། |

A stem ending in the vowel-suffix འ takes འོ་ with contraction (དཀའོ།, མངའོ།);
a stem with no suffix consonant takes འོ་ appended directly (སྡེའོ།, པོའོ།,
མའོ།). A sentence closing with one of these particles is followed by a **double
shad (།།)**, never a single shad — གཏོང་ངོ་།། not གཏོང་ངོ།, and never a
mismatched form like གཏོང་དོ། (ང suffix wrongly paired with the དོ particle,
which belongs to a ད suffix). This does not apply to the auxiliary/copula verbs
ཡིན་, མིན་, ཡོད་, མེད་, which conventionally close bare with a single shad. It
never applies to fixed liturgy or verse text, which is reproduced exactly as
attested regardless of its own grammar.

Checking a generated sentence against this table is **mechanical cleanup, not
content authorship** — correct a mismatch directly rather than regenerating.

### Contamination from a neighbouring language

Tibetan and Dzongkha share script and vocabulary but diverge in grammar and
idiom. Common contamination patterns:

| ❌ Dzongkha form | ✓ Tibetan equivalent |
|---|---|
| བགྱི་ (as 1st-person verb) | བྱ་ / བྱེད་ / བྱ་རྒྱུ་ / བྱ་བ་ཡིན་ |
| གནད་དུ་ཕབ་ + བསམ་གཏན་ | ཡིད་གཏད་དེ / སེམས་བཞག་སྟེ / དྲན་པ་གཏད་དེ |
| ལགས་ / ལགས་སོ། (as sentence-final particle) | ཡིན་ནོ།། / འགྱུར་རོ།། |

If it is unclear whether a phrase belongs to the neighbouring language,
restructure using simpler attested constructions rather than risk contamination.

### Register

Target audience: practitioners already familiar with the text. Refer to the
author and to the teacher by the forms the termbase fixes, in full on first
mention and short thereafter.
